## 0. The big picture

The conceptual progression is:

$$
\text{MLE}\longrightarrow\text{Latent-variable inference}\longrightarrow
\text{VI / ELBO}\longrightarrow\text{Amortized inference}
\overset{\text{reparameterization}}{\longrightarrow}\text{VAE training}.
$$

A variational autoencoder is **not just an autoencoder with random noise**. It combines a probabilistic generative model with a trainable approximate posterior. The original paper primarily addresses scalable *learning and inference* in models with continuous latent variables and intractable posteriors—not simply the problem of making an ordinary autoencoder generate pictures.

**Notation at a glance**

| Symbol | Role |
|---|---|
| $x$ | Observed data (e.g. a $28\times28$ digit image) |
| $z$ | Latent variable |
| $p(z)$ | Prior used to generate new $z$ values |
| $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | Generative likelihood; decoder |
| $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ | True posterior, usually intractable |
| $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ | Approximate posterior; encoder / recognition model |
| $\theta$ | Decoder network weights and biases |
| $\phi$ | Encoder network weights and biases |
| $(\mu_\phi(x),\log\sigma_\phi^2(x))$ | Encoder outputs for the chosen Gaussian family |

## 1. Motivation: why MLE and classical EM become difficult

Assume a generative latent-variable model:

$$
 z\sim p(z),\qquad x\mid z\sim p_\theta(x\mid z),\qquad
 p_\theta(x,z)=p(z)p_\theta(x\mid z).
$$

Given $x_1,\ldots,x_N$, the statistical goal is marginal maximum likelihood:

$$
\theta^*=\arg\max_\theta\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(x_i),\qquad
p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz.
$$

For nonlinear neural-network decoders, the marginalization over $z$ usually has no convenient closed form. The exact posterior is difficult for the same reason:

$$
p_\theta(z\mid x)=\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)}{p_\theta(x)}.
$$

In **exact EM**, the E-step computes $p_{\theta^{\rm old}}(z\mid x)$ or the expectations it requires; the M-step updates $\theta$. When this posterior is intractable, the exact E-step becomes difficult. **Variational EM** instead optimizes an approximate posterior, sometimes using coordinate ascent (CAVI) and sometimes gradients.

The original VAE paper proposes a method that can use minibatches, Monte Carlo gradients, and a shared recognition network to address this setting.

## 2. ELBO derivation: the training objective

Introduce an approximate posterior $q_\phi(z\mid x)$, with support compatible with the joint model. Multiplying and dividing inside the marginal-likelihood integral gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(x)
&=\log\int q_\phi(z\mid x)
 \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,dz\\
&=\log\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
 \left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]\\
&\geq \mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

where the final inequality is **Jensen's inequality** for the concave logarithm. The right-hand side is the evidence lower bound:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)
=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)].}
$$

Using $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)$:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)
=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
 [\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]}_{\text{reconstruction / expected log-likelihood}}
-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)\right)}_{\text{regularization}}.
}
$$

An equivalent identity, useful for understanding the bound, is:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)-\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)
=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x)\right)\geq0.
$$

Thus we **maximize the ELBO** (or equivalently **minimize the negative ELBO**):

$$
\boxed{J(\theta,\phi;x)=-\mathcal L
=-\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)).}
$$

Two precise points:

1. The ELBO is a **lower bound**, not the minimum value of $\log p_\theta(x)$.
2. Maximizing the joint $\mathcal L(\theta,\phi)$ need not increase the exact marginal log-likelihood at every optimizer step. For fixed $\theta$, a tighter ELBO corresponds to a better approximation to that model's posterior.

### Why not use Monte Carlo directly on $\log p_\theta(x)$?

We can sample $z\sim p(z)$ to approximate $p_\theta(x)=\mathbb E_{p(z)}[p_\theta(x\mid z)]$, but taking the log of a finite-sample estimate is biased, and prior samples can be inefficient for a particular observed $x$. The approximate posterior $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ makes the bound more useful by focusing inference on latent values that help explain the observation.

## 3. VAE architecture: three different distributions

A common VAE makes these choices:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(z)&=\mathcal N(0,I),\\
q_\phi(z\mid x)&=\mathcal N\left(\mu_\phi(x),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))\right),\\
p_\theta(x\mid z)&=\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\pi_\theta(z)\right)
\quad\text{(for binary pixels).}
\end{aligned}
$$

The encoder **takes $x$**, predicts parameters $(\mu,\log\sigma^2)$, and then draws a latent $z$. It does *not* take $z$ as input to predict those parameters.

The decoder neural network typically returns **logits** $a_\theta(z)$, with $\pi_\theta(z)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_\theta(z))$. These are the parameters of a conditional Bernoulli distribution. Displaying $\pi_\theta(z)$ as a grayscale image shows the conditional mean; drawing $x_j\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_j)$ gives a binary sample.

### Training versus generation

| Stage | Computation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Training / reconstruction** | $x\to q_\phi(z\mid x)\to z\to p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | Learn inference and generation jointly |
| **Unconditional generation** | $z\sim p(z)\to p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | No observed $x$; encoder not needed |
| **Posterior-mean reconstruction** | $x\to\mu_\phi(x)\to\pi_\theta(\mu)$ | Stable visual diagnostic, **not** an MC estimate of the ELBO |

### Why standard normal?

$\mathcal N(0,I)$ is easy to sample, has full support on $\mathbb R^d$, and gives a closed-form KL when $q_\phi$ is diagonal Gaussian. It is a **convenient modelling assumption, not a requirement that the observed images be Gaussian**. A flexible decoder can map a simple prior to a complex or multimodal data distribution. Mixture or Student-$t$ priors are also possible, though KL computation and optimization may become harder.

## 4. Why MC + reparameterization are separate ideas

The reconstruction expectation is often intractable:

$$
\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
=\int q_\phi(z\mid x)\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz.
$$

A nonlinear decoder makes this expectation hard to integrate analytically. **Monte Carlo** estimates it with $K$ samples:

$$
\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
\approx\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\log p_\theta(x\mid z^{(k)}),
\quad z^{(k)}\sim q_\phi(z\mid x).
$$

**Reparameterization** addresses a different issue: getting a low-variance gradient with respect to the encoder parameters $\phi$ through the sampling operation.

For a Gaussian encoder:

$$
\boxed{\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad
z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon.}
$$

With $\epsilon$ independent of $\phi$, the sampled $z$ is now a differentiable function of $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial\mu}=1,
\qquad\frac{\partial z}{\partial\sigma}=\epsilon.
$$

```python
std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
eps = torch.randn_like(std)
z = mu + std * eps   # differentiable path
```

In PyTorch, `Normal(mu, std).sample()` does not expose a pathwise gradient through the sample, whereas `Normal(mu, std).rsample()` does. This is an implementation distinction, not a claim that the expectation is mathematically nondifferentiable.

### Score-function estimator (contrast)

For $L(\phi)=\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z)}[f(z)]$ and $f$ not explicitly depending on $\phi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\phi L
&=\int f(z)\nabla_\phi q_\phi(z)\,dz\\
&=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\left[f(z)\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

This **likelihood-ratio / REINFORCE estimator** does not need a differentiable sampling path, but it often has relatively high variance. For explicit $\phi$-dependence in $f$, add the direct derivative term $\mathbb E_q[\nabla_\phi f]$.

**Minimal worked check.** If $Z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,1)$ and $L(\mu)=\mathbb E[Z^2]$, then

$$
L(\mu)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)+(\mathbb EZ)^2=1+\mu^2,
\qquad \frac{dL}{d\mu}=2\mu.
$$

A single-sample `loss = z**2` already has expectation $\mu^2+1$; **do not add `+1` to that sample loss**. Reparameterization yields a pathwise gradient $2z$; the score-function form gives $z^2(z-\mu)$.

## 5. The Gaussian KL in closed form

For $q_\phi(z\mid x)=\mathcal N(\mu,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$ and $p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)$:

$$
\boxed{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi\Vert p)
=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^d\left(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\right).
}
$$

This is why standard implementations often use **SGVB estimator B**: Monte Carlo reconstruction + exact Gaussian KL, rather than sampling both terms.

```python
kl_per_image = 0.5 * (
    mu.square() + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar
).sum(dim=1)
kl_loss = kl_per_image.mean()
```

The most important shape rule is: **sum across latent dimensions, then mean across images**. If `mu.shape == (B, D)`, then `sum(dim=1)` produces one KL value per image.

**Two SGVB estimators** in the original paper:

$$
\widehat{\mathcal L}_A
=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\left[
\log p_\theta(x,z^{(k)})-\log q_\phi(z^{(k)}\mid x)
\right],
$$

$$
\widehat{\mathcal L}_B
=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\log p_\theta(x\mid z^{(k)})
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)).
$$

Both estimate the ELBO. B removes MC noise from the **KL term**, but the reconstruction gradient remains stochastic. Using the same $\epsilon$ in both estimators is a good technique for a fair gradient-variance comparison.

## 6. Amortized inference: the bridge from VI to VAE

**Per-data-point VI:**

$$
\lambda_i^*\in\arg\max_{\lambda_i}\mathcal L(\theta,\lambda_i;x_i).
$$

For a diagonal Gaussian, $\lambda_i=(\mu_i,\log\sigma_i^2)$; classical VI may iteratively optimize these local parameters for every $x_i$.

**Amortized VI:**

$$
\boxed{\lambda_i=f_\phi(x_i).}
$$

One shared network predicts local variational parameters for any new observation. The network amortizes the cost of inference across many data points. The concept predates the VAE paper and does not require a neural network: a conditional frequency table or logistic regression could also be used as a shared inference rule.

For example, a lookup table might encode $q(z=1\mid x=1)=0.8$; it returns a Bernoulli **parameter**, not an optimized ELBO value. We sample $z$ from that distribution only if a subsequent computation requires a sampled latent.

### Approximation gap vs amortization gap

At fixed $\theta$, let $q^*$ be the best distribution within the chosen family $\mathcal Q$. Then

$$
\underbrace{\log p_\theta(x)-\mathcal L(q_\phi)}_{\text{inference gap}}
=\underbrace{\log p_\theta(x)-\mathcal L(q^*)}_{\text{approximation gap}}
+\underbrace{\mathcal L(q^*)-\mathcal L(q_\phi)}_{\text{amortization gap}}.
$$

- **Approximation gap:** even the best diagonal Gaussian might fail to approximate a multimodal posterior.
- **Amortization gap:** the encoder's prediction may be worse than a Gaussian optimized for that particular image.

**Semi-amortized inference:** initialize $\lambda_0=f_\phi(x)$, then locally refine:

$$
\lambda_{t+1}=\lambda_t+\eta\nabla_{\lambda_t}\mathcal L(\theta,\lambda_t;x),
\qquad\text{with }\theta\text{ held fixed}.
$$

This is E-step-like local posterior inference. It differs from **latent MAP optimization**, which optimizes a particular vector $z$ rather than the parameters of an approximate posterior distribution. See Cremer, Li & Duvenaud (ICML 2018) in References.

## 7. Likelihood choice: why MSE or BCE?

A decoder must define the likelihood $p_\theta(x\mid z)$, not merely a reconstruction array.

### Gaussian likelihood and MSE

If $p_\theta(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(z),\sigma_x^2 I)$ with **fixed** $\sigma_x^2$:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)
=\frac{\|x-\mu_\theta(z)\|^2}{2\sigma_x^2}
+\frac d2\log(2\pi\sigma_x^2).
$$

For fixed variance, minimizing this negative log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing squared error **for the reconstruction term**. In the full VAE objective, its scaling relative to KL still matters.

### Bernoulli likelihood and BCE

For binary $x_j\in\{0,1\}$:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)
= -\sum_j\left[x_j\log\pi_j+(1-x_j)\log(1-\pi_j)\right],
\qquad\pi_j=\sigma(a_j).
$$

This is per-pixel BCE. Compute it stably from logits:

```python
recon = torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
    logits, x, reduction="none"
)
recon_loss = recon.flatten(start_dim=1).sum(dim=1).mean()
```

**Dataset subtlety.** Our exercises used `transforms.ToTensor()` to get MNIST intensities in $[0,1]$. BCE accepts these fractional targets and is useful in practice, but a *strict Bernoulli likelihood over observed binary pixels* calls for binarized MNIST. Dynamic binarization is one option:

```python
x = torch.bernoulli(x)   # for a binary-pixel experiment, during training
```

For a rigorously modelled continuous grayscale likelihood, use an appropriate continuous distribution instead. Changing preprocessing changes the experiment, so do not silently compare the resulting NLL with previous runs.

### Why mean reconstructions can look blurry

The minimum of $\mathbb E[(X-a)^2\mid z]$ occurs at $a=\mathbb E[X\mid z]$. For a pixel that is 0 or 1 with probability $1/2$, the best deterministic squared-error prediction is $0.5$—a gray value. When several different images are compatible with the same latent code, their conditional mean can have blurred or doubled edges.

However, showing **decoder means** and **sampling from decoder likelihoods** are not the same operation. Binary Bernoulli samples can look noisy, not necessarily sharper in a perceptually meaningful way.

## 8. Minimal PyTorch implementation (our MLP VAE)

The following is the compact, runnable baseline we used conceptually in the coding lab. It uses a single hidden layer and returns **logits** from the decoder.

### `model.py`

```python
import torch
from torch import nn


class Encoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim=784, hidden_dim=400, latent_dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.fc_mu = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, latent_dim)
        self.fc_logvar = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, latent_dim)
        self.relu = nn.ReLU()

    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.relu(self.fc1(x))
        return self.fc_mu(h), self.fc_logvar(h)


class Decoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent_dim=2, hidden_dim=400, output_dim=784):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(latent_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, output_dim)
        self.relu = nn.ReLU()

    def forward(self, z):
        h = self.relu(self.fc1(z))
        return self.fc2(h)  # logits, not probabilities


class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim=784, hidden_dim=400, latent_dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = Encoder(input_dim, hidden_dim, latent_dim)
        self.decoder = Decoder(latent_dim, hidden_dim, input_dim)
        self.latent_dim = latent_dim

    @staticmethod
    def reparameterize(mu, logvar):
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)
        return mu + std * eps

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.encoder(x)
        z = self.reparameterize(mu, logvar)
        logits = self.decoder(z)
        return logits, mu, logvar
```

### `loss.py`

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F


def vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar, beta=1.0):
    # Sum pixel NLL per image; average over the batch.
    recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        logits, x, reduction="none"
    ).flatten(1).sum(dim=1).mean()

    # Sum KL over latent dimensions; average over the batch.
    kl = 0.5 * (
        mu.square() + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar
    ).sum(dim=1).mean()

    loss = recon + beta * kl
    return loss, recon, kl
```

### `train.py` (core loop)

```python
import torch
from torchvision import datasets, transforms
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torch.utils.tensorboard import SummaryWriter
from model import VAE
from loss import vae_loss


device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
train_data = datasets.MNIST(
    root="./data", train=True, download=True,
    transform=transforms.ToTensor()
)
loader = DataLoader(train_data, batch_size=128, shuffle=True)
model = VAE(latent_dim=2).to(device)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
writer = SummaryWriter("runs/vae_baseline")

for epoch in range(1, 11):
    model.train()
    running = torch.zeros(3, dtype=torch.float64)

    for x, _ in loader:
        x = x.flatten(start_dim=1).to(device)
        logits, mu, logvar = model(x)
        loss, recon, kl = vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar)

        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

        b = x.shape[0]
        running += torch.tensor([loss.item(), recon.item(), kl.item()]) * b

    epoch_mean = running / len(train_data)
    total, recon, kl = epoch_mean.tolist()
    writer.add_scalar("Train/Total", total, epoch)
    writer.add_scalar("Train/Recon", recon, epoch)
    writer.add_scalar("Train/KL", kl, epoch)
    print(f"Epoch {epoch}: total={total:.2f}, recon={recon:.2f}, KL={kl:.2f}")

writer.close()
torch.save(model.state_dict(), "vae.pth")
```

**Programming notes.** `nn.Module` automatically registers submodules' weights; $\phi$ and $\theta$ are *mathematical names* for those weights, not required Python variables. `loss.backward()` computes gradients; `optimizer.step()` updates the parameters; `model.train()` only switches training-mode behaviour (relevant for Dropout/BatchNorm). `torch.no_grad()` suppresses graph construction during inference; `model.eval()` does not.


### 9.3 Latent-dimension ablation: 2 vs 10 vs 20

![Same images reconstructed with 2, 10, 20 dimensional VAEs](/assets/images/generative-models/latent_dim_reconstruction.png)

*Figure 3 — Same eight images, original followed by 2D / 10D / 20D reconstructions. Reconstruction visually improves with more dimensions, but prior-sample quality is a separate question.*

In the ideal globally optimized *nested* model families, extra dimensions can be ignored by setting their encoder marginals to $\mathcal N(0,1)$ and letting the decoder ignore them; hence the best achievable ELBO cannot worsen merely because more dimensions were allowed. **Actual optimization and test performance are not monotonic guarantees.** MNIST has 10 labels, but class count is not the same as latent degrees of freedom: stroke thickness, tilt and style vary within each digit class.

An early ablation printed test loss roughly twice training loss because reconstruction/KL were inadvertently returned with inconsistent batch normalization. We corrected the convention: **sum over features, mean over batch**. Do not interpret the early unnormalized test numbers as comparable per-image NLL.

*Figure 5 — Same prior seed across different trained decoders. At $\beta=100$ and $1000$, many decoder mean images converge to a very similar blurred digit-like template.*

Interpretation:

- **$\beta=0$:** no KL penalty; $\sigma\approx0.0027$ and large across-image variance in encoder means. The model nearly uses deterministic codes; sampling from $\mathcal N(0,I)$ is not guaranteed to match the encoded-data region.
- **$\beta=1$:** standard VAE objective with a reconstruction–rate trade-off.
- **$\beta=100$ or $1000$:** KL $\approx0$, $\sigma\approx1$, encoder means nearly independent of $x$, and decoder outputs nearly invariant in the visualized means—strong evidence of posterior collapse.

A blurred, repeated 8-like shape at extreme $\beta$ should not be interpreted as a learned preference for the digit 8. With a pixel-factorized decoder ignoring $z$, the BCE-optimal conditional mean resembles the **per-pixel average of the MNIST training set**.

**Mutual-information interpretation.** Define the *aggregate posterior* $q_\phi(z)=\int p_{\rm data}(x)q_\phi(z\mid x)\,dx$. Then

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{p_{\rm data}(x)}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z))
=I_q(X;Z)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z)\Vert p(z)).
}
$$

The average KL penalizes **both** the information $Z$ carries about $X$ and aggregate-prior mismatch. Since $I_q(X;Z)=I_q(Z;X)$, saying “$x$ reveals nothing about $z$” and “$z$ reveals nothing about $x$” are equivalent under this joint distribution. Full collapse $q(z\mid x)=p(z)$ for all $x$ gives both terms zero.

Importantly, **$q(z)=p(z)$ alone does not imply collapse**; an informative conditional posterior can aggregate to the prior. Also, larger $\beta$ does not *always* induce collapse.

### 9.6 Conditional VAE: conditioning on the digit label

The teaching implementation uses an additional one-hot class vector $y\in\{0,1\}^{10}$:

$$
q_\phi(z\mid x,y),\qquad p_\theta(x\mid z,y).
$$

```python
y_onehot = torch.nn.functional.one_hot(y, num_classes=10).float()
encoder_input = torch.cat([x, y_onehot], dim=1)   # (B, 794)
decoder_input = torch.cat([z, y_onehot], dim=1)   # (B, D+10)
```

In a linear layer $W[z;y]+b=W_z z+W_y y+b$, multiplying by one-hot $y=e_c$ selects column $c$ of $W_y$. This is valid even though $z$ is continuous and $y$ is categorical: concatenation joins **features**, not probability distributions.

![CVAE conditional generation](/assets/images/generative-models/cvae_conditional_generation.png)

*Figure 6 — Our 10-by-8 conditional MNIST output: rows specify digits 0–9; columns vary $z$. Training ended at test negative ELBO $\approx101.71$ (reconstruction $\approx79.70$, KL $\approx22.01$). Conditional control is visible, though not every sample is perfect.*

The simple label-concatenation CVAE is a teaching construction, not a claim that concatenating one-hot labels alone constitutes the complete contribution of the 2015 structured-output conditional generative-model paper.

### 9.7 Image completion: optimize $z$, not network weights

![MNIST half-masked completion](/assets/images/generative-models/image_completion.png)

*Figure 7 — Original images, half-masked inputs, direct encoder reconstruction, and optimized latent completion. The visible pixels are preserved; the missing region is inferred, not observed.*

We kept the trained encoder and decoder fixed, used the encoder on a masked image to initialize $z$, and then optimized **the latent vector itself**:

$$
\boxed{
 z^*=\arg\min_z
 \left\{
 \sum_{j:M_j=1}\mathrm{BCE}(x_j,a_{\theta,j}(z))
 +\frac\lambda2\|z\|^2
 \right\}.
}
$$

The mask is applied to **pixels $x$**, not coordinates of $z$. Only visible pixels contribute to the data-fit term. The $\|z\|^2$ term is the negative log of the standard Gaussian prior up to a constant. Finally,

$$
x_{\rm completed}=M\odot x+(1-M)\odot\sigma(a_\theta(z^*)).
$$

```python
# Decoder weights are fixed, but gradients still flow THROUGH it to z.
for p in model.parameters():
    p.requires_grad_(False)

z = mu.detach().clone().requires_grad_(True)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam([z], lr=0.05)

for _ in range(200):
    logits = model.decoder(z)
    pixel_bce = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        logits, masked_flat, reduction="none"
    )
    observed = (pixel_bce * mask_flat).sum(dim=1).mean()
    prior = 0.5 * z.square().sum(dim=1).mean()
    loss = observed + prior
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

This is **MAP-style optimization of a point $z$**, not VI over a Gaussian $q_\lambda(z)$. Good visible-pixel matching does not guarantee the truly hidden pixels are recovered: the completion can be ambiguous.

### 9.8 Latent dimension activity

The diagnostic computes the *average KL per coordinate* and the across-image variance of encoder means:

$$
\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_j=\frac1N\sum_i\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z_j\mid x_i)\Vert\mathcal N(0,1)),
\qquad v_j=\mathrm{Var}_{x}(\mu_{\phi,j}(x)).
$$

Our heuristic threshold of $0.1$ nat classified all coordinates as “active”:

| Model | Dimensions above $0.1$ nat | Total KL (nats) | Mean $v_j$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| VAE-2 | 2/2 | 5.79 | 1.69 |
| VAE-10 | 10/10 | 18.26 | 1.10 |
| VAE-20 | 20/20 | 25.01 | 0.92 |

### 9.10 Local variational refinement (semi-amortized diagnostic)

![Local variational optimization negative ELBO](/assets/images/generative-models/semi_amortized_elbo.png)

*Figure 10 — With decoder weights fixed, optimizing per-image $\mu$ and $\log\sigma^2$ lowered estimated negative ELBO from about $103.0$ to $100.1$ (improvement $\approx2.9$ nats per image).* 

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    mu0, logvar0 = model.encoder(x)

mu = mu0.detach().clone().requires_grad_(True)
logvar = logvar0.detach().clone().requires_grad_(True)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam([mu, logvar], lr=0.03)

# Each step: sample eps, estimate negative ELBO with mu/logvar,
# backward(), then optimizer.step() to update the local variational parameters.
# Decoder weights stay fixed.
```

Initial rapid improvement followed by a plateau shows that an encoder prediction can be improved by local optimization. It estimates *recoverable amortization error*, but a finite sequence of gradient steps is not a guarantee of reaching the best possible variational approximation. A fully trained **semi-amortized VAE** can additionally differentiate through or otherwise incorporate the local refinement during training.

### 9.11 CNN VAE and interactive explorer: coding extensions

**Convolutional VAE.** Replaces MLP layers with `Conv2d` and `ConvTranspose2d`, using shapes:

$$
(B,1,28,28)\rightarrow(B,32,14,14)\rightarrow(B,64,7,7)
\rightarrow(B,D),
$$

then reversing the spatial resolutions in the decoder. With $k=4$, stride $s=2$, padding $p=1$:

$$
H_{\rm out}=\left\lfloor\frac{H+2p-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1,
$$

so $28\to14\to7$. A ConvVAE is useful preparation for the U-Net used in DDPM, but its final run outcome was not included in the confirmed results here.

**Interactive explorer.** Load the 2D decoder and call `decode(z_1,z_2)` from a Matplotlib slider callback or mouse-click event. Each UI action changes the latent point and refreshes the decoder mean image. The implementation was supplied but execution was not confirmed at the time of writing.

**Alternative priors / WAE-MMD.** Deferred until the Optimal Transport module. WAE is not synonymous with MMD: WAE-MMD uses a kernel discrepancy to match the **aggregated** encoded distribution $q(z)$ to $p(z)$, while WAE-GAN uses adversarial matching. Its objective is not the standard VAE ELBO.

## 10. Debugging and numerical checklist

1. **Tensor shapes:** MLP input `(B,784)`; encoder `mu,logvar` `(B,D)`; reparameterized `z` `(B,D)`; decoder logits `(B,784)`. ConvVAE uses `(B,1,28,28)`.
2. **Logits versus probabilities:** return logits from the decoder for `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`; call `sigmoid` only to display pixel means or to use Bernoulli parameters.
3. **Loss reduction:** use `reduction="none"`, sum over pixels / latent dims, then **mean over the batch**. A past mismatch made the larger test batches report incorrectly inflated component losses.
4. **Single-example versus batch metrics:** `len(loader.dataset)` counts images, `len(loader)` counts batches, `x.size(0)` is the current batch size. The last batch may be shorter.
5. **Sampling path:** use `rsample()` or explicit `z=mu+std*eps` to propagate reconstruction gradients to the encoder.
6. **Mode and gradients:** `model.train()` / `.eval()` switch behaviour of mode-sensitive modules; they do not create or disable gradients. Use `torch.no_grad()` when inference truly needs no gradient—but **not** while optimizing $z$ through a frozen decoder.
7. **Weights:** `model.parameters()` recursively includes both encoder and decoder registered layers; `torch.save(model.state_dict(), ...)` stores parameters; `torch.load(..., weights_only=True)` restricts supported deserialization types, and **does not mean “weights without biases.”**
8. **Reproducibility:** log seeds, preprocessing, batch size, latent dimension, architecture, optimizer, learning rate, and evaluation $K$. Keep the same images for qualitative reconstruction comparisons and do not over-interpret small sample grids.
9. **Fair comparison:** a 2D vs 20D VAE, or a label-conditioned CVAE vs unconditional VAE, does not isolate every modelling factor. The comparison should name which variables differ.

## 11. Open questions and the route to DDPM

We now have a clear model of where VAE succeeds and fails:

- **Posterior approximation** can be restricted by the variational family and amortization.
- **KL weighting** trades reconstruction fidelity against latent rate and prior compatibility; overly strong pressure can induce collapse.
- **A pixel-factorized likelihood** can produce blurry conditional mean images when uncertainty is multimodal.
- **Generation and reconstruction are different tests:** the former samples from a prior; the latter conditions on an observed input.

The next project is **DDPM**. In a VAE we sample one latent $z$ and decode; in a diffusion model we define a chain of latent-noise states and learn a reverse denoising process. The familiar concepts return: Gaussian conditional distributions, reparameterized noise, Monte Carlo expectations, ELBOs, and MSE-based objectives.

A key DDPM identity to derive next is:

$$
\boxed{x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).}
$$

This gives arbitrary-time forward noising without iterating through every previous time step. The immediate prerequisite is Gaussian algebra and Markov-chain transitions; continuous-time SDEs and Optimal Transport can follow later.
